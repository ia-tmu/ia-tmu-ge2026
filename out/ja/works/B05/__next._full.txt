1c:T448,Scat singing in a cappella performance is characterized by the use of meaningless syllables, in which vocal timbre and nuance are shaped through bodily actions such as mouth opening, articulation, and continuous vowel transitions. In contrast, timbral variation in piano performance is often treated as a preconfigured or external process, separated from the performer’s bodily actions during performance.

In this project, an interactive system was developed to capture the degree of mouth opening during singing using a web camera and map this data in real time to effect parameters applied to an electronic piano. By focusing on the continuity of vowel articulation, multiple patterns of timbral change were designed to enable gradual and continuous transitions in piano sound.

Through experimental performances comparing different mapping patterns, the results indicate that timbral changes became perceptible as part of the performer’s bodily actions. The installation proposes the creation of a hybrid ecosystem where the physical and the digital intertwine. Physical artifacts—such as stones, everyday objects, and gardening tools—serve as interfaces for a living digital world. The "Urban Garden Database," an exploratory storage of information compiled during the research phase, was fundamental to understanding and mapping the imagery, flora, and fauna of these spaces. While not technically linked to the ecosystem's operation, this mapping provided the conceptual foundation for translating the garden's complexity into the simulated environment.

Unlike conventional technological interfaces that prioritize immediate response and total user control, this work adopts the principles of Slow Technology. The system is governed by stochastic (probabilistic) models, meaning the digital ecosystem possesses its own agency and temporality. The audience is invited to a patient observation: by positioning objects, one influences but does not dominate the growth and dynamics of the simulated species. The system seeks to reflect the opacity and interdependence of living systems.

This research invites the public to rethink technology not as a tool for exploitation, but as a medium for subjectivity and care, capable of reconnecting us with the invisible complexities of the urban ecology that surrounds us.
