1c:T754,In a metropolis like Tokyo, where space is a scarce resource and urban density often suffocates the horizon, nature survives in fragments: in the small domestic gardens that sprout between asphalt and concrete. This work is the result of research investigating how interactive art can mediate our ecological awareness through these spaces of "everyday nature."

The installation proposes the creation of a hybrid ecosystem where the physical and the digital intertwine. Physical artifacts—such as stones, everyday objects, and gardening tools—serve as interfaces for a living digital world. The "Urban Garden Database," an exploratory storage of information compiled during the research phase, was fundamental to understanding and mapping the imagery, flora, and fauna of these spaces. While not technically linked to the ecosystem's operation, this mapping provided the conceptual foundation for translating the garden's complexity into the simulated environment.

Unlike conventional technological interfaces that prioritize immediate response and total user control, this work adopts the principles of Slow Technology. The system is governed by stochastic (probabilistic) models, meaning the digital ecosystem possesses its own agency and temporality. The audience is invited to a patient observation: by positioning objects, one influences but does not dominate the growth and dynamics of the simulated species. The system seeks to reflect the opacity and interdependence of living systems.

This research invites the public to rethink technology not as a tool for exploitation, but as a medium for subjectivity and care, capable of reconnecting us with the invisible complexities of the urban ecology that surrounds us.
